    about the data

    where it comes from

    The history is ERA5, the reanalysis made by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts for the Copernicus Climate Change Service. It rebuilds every hour since 1940 from stations, balloons, ships and satellites.

    It is served, free and open, by Open-Meteo. The radar is a mosaic of national weather radars gathered by RainViewer, on a map from OpenStreetMap and CARTO. Places come from GeoNames.

    how to read it

    Each dot is one hour, day, week, month or year. Colours stretch across each place’s own range, so a deep orange in Fairbanks is not the same heat as one in Houston. Hover or tap a dot for its number.

    Change colours each cell by how far it sat from the same time of year in 1951–1980, the baseline NASA uses for its global temperature record. Blue and ochre are below normal; red and teal above. The years view draws them as warming stripes, after the climate scientist Ed Hawkins.

    Field lets neighbouring cells bleed into one another, like pigment, so slow patterns show through the day-to-day noise. Switch to dots for the exact values. Save image (or the S key) draws what you see again at print size, with the place and the numbers in the corner, and shows you a preview before it downloads. Quiet (the switch in the corner, or the Q key) puts the controls away so the picture fills the page; click the place name to bring them back.

    The hairline across the top is the whole planet, every year since 1850, from NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information.

    The radar view shows only rain, whatever measure you pick, and only where countries share their radar: most of North America, Europe, Japan and Australia, and parts of elsewhere.

    A reanalysis describes a patch of sky about 25 km across, so a dot is the weather of a neighbourhood, not of one thermometer. The newest five days are still being checked and are left out.

    why it matters

    Data often says the most when you can watch it over time. One hot week is just weather. Eighty-six years of weeks side by side start to tell a story.

    Zoom out and the bigger picture shows itself: summers stretching into October, warm years piling up since 2010.
